Chapter 7Time Series Regression: Linear Regression

Chapter 6 forecast a series from its own past: averages, weighted averages, and exponentially weighted averages of $X(t-1), X(t-2), \dots$ Nothing else entered the model. That is a strength and a limitation: the model can never tell you why demand jumped, and it cannot answer "what happens if next summer is 3 degrees hotter?"

Regression puts explanatory variables back in. We model the series of interest $y_t$ as a linear function of one or more predictors $x_{1,t}, \dots, x_{k,t}$, such as temperature, a working-day indicator, a trend, a seasonal dummy, the price of a competing product. The mechanics are ordinary least squares, familiar from any statistics course. What is not ordinary is everything the time ordering brings with it: autocorrelated residuals, spurious regressions between trending series, seasonal predictors we have to build ourselves, and the awkward fact that to forecast $y$ we must first know the future $x$'s.

Regression takes two lectures. This chapter covers the linear machinery: the model and its assumptions, least squares, residual diagnostics, and the predictors we build out of the time index. Chapter 8 takes up model selection, forecasting, nonlinear and piecewise trends, and what correlation is and is not worth.

7.1Learning objectives#

By the end of this lecture you should be able to

  1. write a time series regression in scalar and matrix form, and state the assumptions least squares needs;
  2. fit and read a regression with statsmodels, and say what $R^2$ and $\hat\sigma_e$ do and do not tell you;
  3. run a residual diagnostic (time plot, ACF, Ljung-Box, histogram, residuals vs fitted) and recognize a spurious regression;
  4. build the standard time series predictors yourself: trend, seasonal dummies, Fourier terms, intervention dummies, lagged predictors.

def run_sequence_plot(y, x=None, title="", xlabel="time", ylabel="value",
                      ax=None, figsize=(9, 3), color="blue", **kwargs):
    "Plot a series against time and return the axes."
    if ax is None:
        _, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=figsize)
    if x is None:
        x = getattr(y, "index", np.arange(len(y)))
    ax.plot(x, y, color=color, lw=1, **kwargs)
    ax.set(title=title, xlabel=xlabel, ylabel=ylabel)
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
    return ax

7.3Linear regression model#

7.3.1Simple linear regression#

One predictor. We assume the forecast variable $y$ and the predictor $x$ are related by

$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_t + \varepsilon_t .$$

The coefficients have flat-footed interpretations: $\beta_1$ is the average change in $y$ for a one-unit increase in $x$ (the slope) and $\beta_0$ is the predicted $y$ when $x = 0$, which is meaningful only if $x = 0$ is inside the range of the data. The error $\varepsilon_t$ collects everything else: drivers we did not include, measurement error, and genuine randomness. It is not assumed to be small, only to be unpredictable from $x$.

Example: electricity demand against temperature, for January 2014 (high summer in Melbourne, when air conditioning drives the load).

7.3.2Multiple linear regression#

With $k$ predictors,

$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1,t} + \beta_2 x_{2,t} + \dots + \beta_k x_{k,t} + \varepsilon_t ,$$

and each $\beta_i$ now measures the effect of $x_i$ after accounting for other predictors (a marginal effect), not a raw correlation. The predictors may be measured variables (temperature), constructed variables (a trend), indicators (workday / not), or nonlinear functions of other predictors (temperature squared): the model is linear in the parameters, which is all least squares requires.

7.3.3The assumptions#

Least squares gives sensible estimates when the errors satisfy:

  1. Zero mean, $\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon_t] = 0$; otherwise the forecasts are biased.
  2. Not autocorrelated; otherwise the forecasts are inefficient. There is signal left in the residuals.
  3. Uncorrelated with each predictor; otherwise the coefficients are biased (the classic endogeneity problem).
  4. Useful but not essential: $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathrm{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ with constant variance, needed for the usual prediction intervals and $p$-values.

Assumption 2 is the one time series break routinely.

A warning built into the data. Fit that same one-predictor model to the whole year instead of January and it collapses, because over a full year the relationship is not a line at all.

We will fix the offset with a dummy variable later in this chapter, and the U with degree days or a piecewise fit in Chapter 8.

7.4Least squares estimation#

The coefficients are chosen to minimize the sum of squared errors

$$\mathrm{SSE} = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\varepsilon_t^2 = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(y_t - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_{1,t} - \dots - \beta_k x_{k,t}\bigr)^2 ,$$

which is why the procedure is called ordinary least squares (OLS). Writing the predictors as a matrix $\mathbf{X}$ (a column of ones, then one column per predictor), the minimizer is available in closed form,

$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y},$$

the normal equations. statsmodels solves them for us; here is the same answer from three lines of numpy, worth doing once so the estimator stops being a black box.

In [9]
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(jan)), jan["Temperature"].to_numpy()])   # design matrix
y = jan["Demand"].to_numpy()

beta_hat = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)      # solve; never invert explicitly

print("normal equations :", np.round(beta_hat, 6))
print("statsmodels      :", np.round(fit_jan.params.to_numpy(), 6))

7.4.1Fitted values, $R^2$, and the residual standard error#

The fitted values are $\hat y_t = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_{1,t} + \dots + \hat\beta_k x_{k,t}$ and the residuals are $e_t = y_t - \hat y_t$. Two summaries follow:

$$R^2 = \frac{\sum(\hat y_t - \bar y)^2}{\sum(y_t - \bar y)^2} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}}, \qquad \hat\sigma_e = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T-k-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T} e_t^2}.$$

$R^2$ is the share of variation explained, and equals the squared correlation between $y$ and $\hat y$. Two cautions, both important for forecasting:

  • $R^2$ never decreases when you add a predictor, even a column of random numbers. It cannot be used to choose between models of different size.
  • A high in-sample $R^2$ says nothing about out-of-sample accuracy, and with trending series it can be high for no reason at all (spurious regression, next section).

$\hat\sigma_e$ is in the units of $y$ and is the quantity that sets the width of prediction intervals. Note the $T-k-1$ divisor: $k+1$ degrees of freedom were spent estimating the coefficients.

In [10]
resid = fit_jan.resid
sse   = float((resid ** 2).sum())
sst   = float(((y - y.mean()) ** 2).sum())
T, k  = int(fit_jan.nobs), int(fit_jan.df_model)

7.5Evaluating the regression model#

A fitted line is a hypothesis, and the residuals are where it is tested. For time series regression we look at five things, and the first two matter most.

Plot What a problem looks like What it means
Residuals against time Waves, runs of same-sign residuals Autocorrelation -- signal left on the table
ACF of residuals + Ljung-Box Spikes outside the bounds, small $p$-value The same thing, quantified
Histogram of residuals Skew, fat tails Prediction intervals will be wrong
Residuals against each predictor Curvature The relationship is nonlinear; transform or add a term
Residuals against fitted values Funnel shape Heteroscedasticity; take logs or model the variance

Autocorrelated residuals do not bias the coefficients (forecasts stay valid on average) but they make prediction intervals too narrow, which is the error a decision maker notices.

In [11]
def residual_diagnostics(fit, lags=10, title="", figsize=(11, 6)):
    "Four-panel residual check plus a Ljung-Box test."
    e = pd.Series(np.asarray(fit.resid, dtype=float))

    fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=figsize)
    fig.suptitle(title or "residual diagnostics")

    run_sequence_plot(e.to_numpy(), title="residuals over time", xlabel="observation",
                      ylabel="residual", ax=axes[0, 0])
    axes[0, 0].axhline(0, color="black", lw=0.8)

    plot_acf(e, lags=lags, ax=axes[0, 1], title="ACF of residuals")

    axes[1, 0].hist(e, bins=15, color="blue", alpha=0.7)
    axes[1, 0].set(title="histogram of residuals", xlabel="residual", ylabel="count")
    axes[1, 0].grid(alpha=0.3)

    axes[1, 1].scatter(fit.fittedvalues, e, s=18, color="blue")
    axes[1, 1].axhline(0, color="black", lw=0.8)
    axes[1, 1].set(title="residuals vs fitted", xlabel="fitted value", ylabel="residual")
    axes[1, 1].grid(alpha=0.3)

    fig.tight_layout()

    # Ljung-Box: H0 = residuals are white noise, allowing for the estimated coefficients
    lb = acorr_ljungbox(e, lags=[lags], model_df=int(fit.df_model))
    stat, pval = float(lb["lb_stat"].iloc[0]), float(lb["lb_pvalue"].iloc[0])
    verdict = "no evidence of autocorrelation" if pval > 0.05 else "RESIDUALS ARE AUTOCORRELATED"
    print(f"Ljung-Box (lag {lags}): Q = {stat:.2f}, p = {pval:.4g}  ->  {verdict}")
    return fit

7.5.1Spurious regression#

Now the failure mode that makes time series regression different from cross-sectional regression. Two series that are each non-stationary and completely independent of one another will, more often than not, produce a regression with a large $R^2$ and an overwhelmingly significant slope.

Below: two independent random walks. Neither knows the other exists.

In [14]
residual_diagnostics(spurious, lags=10, title="spurious regression: the residuals give it away");

The $p$-value says the slope is real beyond any doubt. It is not, as we generated the two series independently. The tell is entirely in the residuals: the ACF decays slowly from near 1, the residual plot wanders, and Ljung-Box rejects white noise by a vast margin. A highly significant coefficient together with strongly autocorrelated residuals is the signature of a spurious regression.

7.6Useful predictors#

Many of the most valuable predictors in a time series regression are not measured variables at all.

7.6.1Trend#

A linear trend is the predictor $x_t = t$, $t = 1, 2, \dots, T$:

$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \varepsilon_t .$$

The coefficient $\beta_1$ is the average change per period. Simple, and dangerous to extrapolate far.

7.6.2Dummy variables#

For a two-valued condition, code a dummy: $1$ when the condition holds, $0$ otherwise (a workday, a holiday, a strike, a product recall, a policy change). A dummy for a single unusual observation (an intervention or outlier dummy) has exactly the effect of dropping that observation from the fit.

A categorical predictor with $m$ categories needs $m-1$ dummies, not $m$. Including all $m$ plus an intercept makes the columns linearly dependent and $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ singular: the dummy variable trap. The omitted category becomes the reference, and each coefficient is a difference from it.

With quarterly data, $m = 4$, so three dummies:

$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \gamma_2 d_{2,t} + \gamma_3 d_{3,t} + \gamma_4 d_{4,t} + \varepsilon_t ,$$

where $d_{i,t} = 1$ if observation $t$ falls in quarter $i$. Q1 is the reference.

7.6.3Fourier terms#

Dummies cost $m-1$ parameters. For long seasonal periods, like $m = 52$ weeks, $m = 365$ days, $m = 48$ half-hours, that is unaffordable. A smoother alternative approximates the seasonal shape with a few sine and cosine pairs (harmonic regression):

$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\alpha_k \sin\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi k t}{m}\Bigr) + \gamma_k \cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi k t}{m}\Bigr)\right] + \varepsilon_t ,$$

with $K \le m/2$. A small $K$ gives a smooth seasonal shape and few parameters; increasing $K$ adds detail. At the maximum $K = m/2$ the Fourier terms span exactly the same space as the seasonal dummies, so the two models are identical. It is easy to check on the beer data ($m = 4$, $K = 2$; the $\sin$ term at $k = 2$ is identically zero and is dropped).

In [20]
def fourier_terms(t, period, K, prefix="f"):
    "Return a DataFrame of K sine/cosine pairs for the given seasonal period."
    t = np.asarray(t, dtype=float)
    cols = {}
    for k in range(1, K + 1):
        cols[f"{prefix}sin{k}"] = np.sin(2 * np.pi * k * t / period)
        cols[f"{prefix}cos{k}"] = np.cos(2 * np.pi * k * t / period)
    out = pd.DataFrame(cols)
    return out.loc[:, out.std() > 1e-12]        # drop terms that are identically zero

7.6.4Other predictors worth knowing#

Predictor Construction Typical use
Intervention dummy $1$ at one date, $0$ elsewhere Strike, recall, outlier -- a spike
Step dummy $0$ before a date, $1$ after Permanent level shift (policy, tariff change)
Piecewise trend $\max(t - \tau, 0)$ Slope change at a knot (Section 7.7)
Trading days Number of Mondays, Tuesdays, ... in the month Monthly sales, production
Holiday dummy $1$ on or near a holiday Easter moves between quarters; retail, transport
Distributed lags $x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, \dots$ Advertising or price changes with delayed effects

All of them are ordinary columns of $\mathbf{X}$; nothing about the estimation changes.

7.7Summary#

Idea Practical form
The model $y_t = \beta_0 + \sum_i \beta_i x_{i,t} + \varepsilon_t$, linear in the parameters
Estimation OLS, $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$; smf.ols(formula, data).fit()
Goodness of fit $R^2$ for description, $\hat\sigma_e$ for interval width, neither for model choice
Diagnostics Residuals vs time, ACF + Ljung-Box, histogram, residuals vs fitted and vs each predictor
The trap Trending series give spurious regressions: big $R^2$, tiny $p$, wildly autocorrelated residuals
Predictors you build Trend $t$; seasonal dummies ($m-1$ of them); Fourier pairs for long $m$; intervention and step dummies; lags
