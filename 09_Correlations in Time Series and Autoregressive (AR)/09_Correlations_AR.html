Chapter 9Correlations in Time Series and Autoregressive (AR) Models

Chapter 8 ended on an unsolved problem: even the best regression left autocorrelation in its residuals. Demand today resembled demand yesterday in a way that no predictor explained. The regression chapters treated that serial dependence as a nuisance. This chapter treats it as the signal.

The idea is simple. If $x_t$ is correlated with its own past, then the past is a predictor, and we can regress the series on itself. This gives two families of models, the building blocks of everything in the ARIMA family:

  • autoregressive (AR) models, in which $x_t$ depends on its own past values;
  • moving-average (MA) models, in which $x_t$ depends on past shocks, the unpredictable part of each past observation.

To decide between them, and to choose how many lags to use, we need two tools: the autocorrelation function (ACF) from Chapters 3 and 4, and a new one, the partial autocorrelation function (PACF).

This chapter builds the two tools and the first family of models, the AR model, and shows how to read its order off the PACF. Chapter 10 puts AR models to work on series with trend and seasonality, and introduces MA models.

9.1Learning objectives#

By the end of this lecture you should be able to

  1. compute and read the ACF, the cross-correlation function (CCF) and the PACF, and explain why the ACF alone cannot separate direct from indirect correlation;
  2. write down an AR($p$) model, say when it is stationary, and translate its coefficients into the statsmodels sign convention;
  3. identify the order of an AR model from the correlograms: the PACF cuts off after lag $p$, while the ACF tails off.

def theory_marks(ax, values, label="theory"):
    "Mark theoretical correlations (lag 0, 1, 2, ...) on a correlogram."
    ax.plot(np.arange(len(values)), values, "_", color="red", ms=14, mew=2, label=label)

def correlograms(x, lags=20, process=None, title=""):
    "ACF and PACF side by side, optionally with the theoretical values of an ArmaProcess."
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 3))
    plot_acf(x, lags=lags, ax=axes[0], title=f"ACF {title}")
    plot_pacf(x, lags=lags, ax=axes[1], title=f"PACF {title}")
    if process is not None:
        theory_marks(axes[0], process.acf(lags + 1))
        theory_marks(axes[1], process.pacf(lags + 1))
    for ax in axes:
        ax.set_xlabel("lag k")
        ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
    fig.tight_layout()
    return axes

9.3Correlations in time series#

A correlation function measures the linear association between two random variables. In a time series, two variables are values at two different times, and this dependence is exactly what a forecasting model exploits.

There are two correlation functions:

  • the autocorrelation function (ACF) compares a series with a lagged copy of itself;
  • the cross-correlation function (CCF) compares a series with a lagged copy of another series.

9.3.1Autocorrelation function (ACF)#

Recall from Chapter 4 that for a stationary process with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the ACF at lag $k$ is

$$\rho(k) = \frac{\mathbb{E}\big[(x_t-\mu)(x_{t-k}-\mu)\big]}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \rho(0) = 1,$$

where $k$ is the lag, $x_t$ is the value at time $t$, and $\mu$ is the mean of the series, and $\sigma^2$ its variance.

Values close to $\pm 1$ mean strong linear dependence between observations $k$ steps apart; values near $0$ mean weak dependence. The definition uses a single $\mu$ and a single $\sigma^2$, so it only makes sense for a stationary series.

A noisy sinusoid of period 10 is a quick first example.

In [4]
n = 100
t = np.arange(n)
time_series_1 = rng.normal(0, 0.5, n) + np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 10)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 3))
run_sequence_plot(time_series_1, title="noisy sinusoid, period 10", ax=axes[0])
plot_acf(time_series_1, lags=20, alpha=0.05, ax=axes[1])   # the first 20 lags; change `lags` for more
axes[1].set(title="ACF", xlabel="lag k")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

How to read the correlogram:

  • Each stem is the sample autocorrelation $\hat\rho(k)$. The series correlates well with itself shifted by one step (lag 1), slightly less at lag 2, and so on.
  • The correlation turns negative around lag 5, half a period, where peaks line up with troughs. It is positive again around lag 10, one full period. Peaks at multiples of the period are the seasonal signature from Chapter 3.
  • The shaded band is a confidence region, here at 95% ($\alpha = 0.05$; other levels can be set). Spikes outside it are statistically significant; spikes inside it are compatible with zero. plot_acf computes the band with Bartlett's formula, so it widens with the lag. Chapter 3 used the simpler fixed band $\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}$. See the documentation for details.

9.3.2Cross-correlation function (CCF)#

The CCF measures the correlation between two series at different lags:

$$\mathrm{CCF}(k) = \frac{\mathbb{E}\big[(x_t-\mu_x)(y_{t-k}-\mu_y)\big]}{\sigma_x\,\sigma_y},$$

where $x$ and $y$ are two different series; $\mu_x, \mu_y$ are their means; and $\sigma_x, \sigma_y$ are their standard deviations. A large $\mathrm{CCF}(k)$ at a positive $k$ says that $y$ leads $x$ by $k$ steps. The CCF is used to locate a short, known pattern inside a long signal, and to find the delay at which a leading indicator is most informative. For example, a load change of a machine shows up in a downstream temperature only some time later.

statsmodels.tsa.stattools.ccf(x, y) follows the same convention: its $k$-th value is the correlation between $x_t$ and $y_{t-k}$. For example, $x$ is a noisy copy of $y$ delayed by 5 steps, and we pretend we do not know the delay.

In [5]
n = 200
driver = ArmaProcess(ar=[1, -0.5]).generate_sample(n, distrvs=rng.standard_normal)   # the input y
delay = 5
response = np.r_[np.zeros(delay), driver[:-delay]] + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)           # x_t = y_{t-5} + noise

ccf_vals = ccf(response, driver, adjusted=False, nlags=21)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 3))
run_sequence_plot(driver[:80], title="input y and response x (first 80 steps)", ax=axes[0], label="input y")
axes[0].plot(response[:80], color="red", lw=1, label="response x")
axes[0].legend(loc="upper right", fontsize=8)

axes[1].stem(np.arange(len(ccf_vals)), ccf_vals, basefmt=" ")
axes[1].axhspan(-1.96 / np.sqrt(n), 1.96 / np.sqrt(n), color="blue", alpha=0.15, label=r"$\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}$")
axes[1].set(title="CCF(k) = corr($x_t$, $y_{t-k}$)", xlabel="lag k", ylabel="CCF", xticks=range(0, 21, 5))
axes[1].legend(loc="upper right", fontsize=8); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print(f"lag of the largest cross-correlation: {np.argmax(ccf_vals)}")

lag of the largest cross-correlation: 5

The CCF peaks at the true delay, $k = 5$. The smaller bars on either side come from the autocorrelation of the input itself: $y_{t-4}$ and $y_{t-6}$ are correlated with $y_{t-5}$, so they are correlated with $x_t$ too. This is the same direct-versus-indirect problem that the PACF solves for a single series, below.

9.3.3Uses and limitations#

Practical uses of correlation functions

  • Identifying the nature of the data: random residuals (e.g., white noise), trending, or seasonal.
  • Choosing the order of AR and MA models, the main subject of this chapter and the next.
  • Checking that model residuals are white noise. The Ljung-Box test of Chapter 7 is built on the residual ACF.

Limitations and considerations

  1. Correlation functions measure only linear relationships.
  2. They are meaningful only for stationary series.
  3. High correlation does not imply causation, and can be misleading (Chapter 8).
  4. The ACF measures direct and indirect correlation together. A strong correlation at a large lag can simply be the accumulation of correlations at shorter lags.

Limitation 4 is easiest to see in a random walk,

$$x_t = x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t .$$

Only lag $k=1$ appears in the equation. But the ACF is significant over many lags, decaying only slowly. Those higher lags are indirect correlations, built up from the single lag-1 link: $x_t$ is tied to $x_{t-1}$, which is tied to $x_{t-2}$, and so on. (A random walk is also nonstationary, so limitation 2 applies too. The slow decay is the "difference me" warning from Chapter 5.)

In [6]
random_walk = np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 1, 100))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 3))
run_sequence_plot(random_walk, title="random walk", ax=axes[0])
plot_acf(random_walk, lags=20, alpha=0.05, ax=axes[1])
axes[1].set(title="ACF of the random walk", xlabel="lag k")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

9.3.4Partial autocorrelation function (PACF)#

The PACF addresses limitation 4 by isolating the direct correlation between a series and its lagged copy. It removes the influence of the observations in between.

The partial autocorrelation at lag $k$ is usually written $\phi_{kk}$:

$$\phi_{kk} = \mathrm{Corr}\big(x_t - \hat x_t,\; x_{t-k} - \hat x_{t-k}\big),$$

where $\hat x_t$ and $\hat x_{t-k}$ are the best linear predictions of $x_t$ and $x_{t-k}$ from the intermediate values $x_{t-1}, \dots, x_{t-k+1}$. Both variables are cleaned of what the in-between lags explain, and only then correlated. For $k=1$, there is nothing in between, so $\phi_{11} = \rho(1)$.

An equivalent and more practical reading: $\phi_{kk}$ is the last coefficient in a regression of $x_t$ on its first $k$ lags,

$$x_t = c + a_1 x_{t-1} + a_2 x_{t-2} + \dots + a_k x_{t-k} + e_t \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \phi_{kk} = a_k .$$

It measures what lag $k$ adds once lags $1, \dots, k-1$ are already in the model. A large $\phi_{kk}$ means a direct link to lag $k$ that the shorter lags do not account for.

Using ACF and PACF together gives a fuller picture:

  • the ACF shows the overall correlation structure, including trend and seasonality;
  • the PACF pinpoints the specific lags that have a significant direct effect on the current value.

Example. Suppose the ACF of a series is significant at lags 1, 2 and 3. Without the PACF, it is unclear whether the lag-3 correlation is direct, or only an echo of the strong correlations at lags 1 and 2. We simulate a series with a genuine direct link at lag 3,

$$x_t = 0.4\,x_{t-1} + 0.2\,x_{t-2} + 0.3\,x_{t-3} + \varepsilon_t ,$$

written out as an explicit loop so that the recursion is visible. We use 500 points, so that the estimates are precise enough to make the point clearly.

In [7]
n = 500
phi1, phi2, phi3 = 0.4, 0.2, 0.3     # coefficients at lags 1, 2 and 3
noise = rng.normal(0, 1, n)

time_series = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(3, n):
    time_series[t] = phi1 * time_series[t-1] + phi2 * time_series[t-2] + phi3 * time_series[t-3] + noise[t]

run_sequence_plot(time_series, title="series with direct correlation at lags 1, 2 and 3", figsize=(12, 3))
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
In [8]
correlograms(time_series, lags=20, title="of the simulated series")
plt.show()

The ACF is large at every lag shown and decays only slowly, so it cannot tell us which lags matter directly. The PACF can: three significant spikes, at lags 1, 2 and 3, and nothing after. Lag 3 is a direct effect, and the large ACF values at lags 4, 5, 6, ... are indirect echoes of the first three. A single spike beyond lag 3 that touches the band would be the occasional false alarm of Chapter 3, expected about once in twenty lags.

To confirm that the PACF really is "the last coefficient of a regression on $k$ lags", compute it by hand with OLS and compare it with statsmodels:

In [9]
def pacf_by_regression(x, k):
    "Last coefficient of an OLS regression of x_t on a constant and x_{t-1}, ..., x_{t-k}."
    X = np.column_stack([x[k - j: len(x) - j] for j in range(1, k + 1)])
    return sm.OLS(x[k:], sm.add_constant(X)).fit().params[-1]


by_hand = [pacf_by_regression(time_series, k) for k in range(1, 7)]
by_ols  = pacf(time_series, nlags=6, method="ols")[1:]
by_ywm  = pacf(time_series, nlags=6, method="ywm")[1:]     # the estimator plot_pacf uses by default

print(" k   by hand (OLS)   pacf(method='ols')   pacf(method='ywm')")
for k, (a, b, c) in enumerate(zip(by_hand, by_ols, by_ywm), start=1):
    print(f"{k:2d}   {a:12.4f}   {b:18.4f}   {c:18.4f}")

 2         0.3466               0.3466               0.3464

The hand-made regressions reproduce pacf(method="ols"). The Yule-Walker estimate that plot_pacf draws by default differs only in the third decimal place. The PACF is nothing more than a sequence of regressions, each asking what does one more lag add?. That is why it is the natural tool for choosing the order of an autoregression, the subject of the next section.

9.4Autoregressive (AR) models#

An autoregressive model uses observations from previous time steps as the inputs of a regression that predicts the next value. The predictors of Chapter 7 are replaced by lagged copies of the series itself, so the model depends solely on its own past.

The general AR model of order $p$, written AR($p$), is

$$x_t = c + \phi_1 x_{t-1} + \phi_2 x_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_p x_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t ,$$

where

  • $x_t$ is the value of the series at time $t$;
  • $c$ is a constant (the intercept);
  • $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p$ are the autoregressive coefficients;
  • $p$ is the order of the model, i.e., the number of lags;
  • $\varepsilon_t$ is white noise with variance $\sigma^2$, called the innovation (or shock) at time $t$.

AR(1). The first-order model

$$x_t = c + \phi_1 x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$$

makes the current value depend on the immediately preceding one. We met it in Chapter 4: it is stationary if and only if $|\phi_1| < 1$, its ACF decays geometrically as $\rho(k) = \phi_1^{\,k}$, and $\phi_1 = 1$ (with $c = 0$) is the random walk.

Example: the temperature of a large thermal mass, sampled every minute. Each reading's deviation from ambient is a fraction of the previous deviation, plus a new random disturbance.

Higher orders. AR(2), AR(3), ... depend on more than one past value. The AR(2) model is

$$x_t = c + \phi_1 x_{t-1} + \phi_2 x_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t ,$$

which is useful when the effect of more than one previous step is significant. For engineers, AR(2) is the discrete-time analogue of a damped second-order oscillator driven by random forcing. When $\phi_1^2 + 4\phi_2 < 0$, the characteristic roots are complex and the series shows pseudo-cycles: oscillations of roughly constant period whose amplitude and phase wander.

Stationarity. An AR($p$) process is stationary when all roots of its characteristic polynomial $1 - \phi_1 z - \phi_2 z^2 - \dots - \phi_p z^p$ lie outside the unit circle. For AR(1), this is $|\phi_1| < 1$; for AR(2) it is the triangle $-1 < \phi_2 < 1$, $\ \phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1$, $\ \phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1$.

ArmaProcess(...).isstationary checks the condition for us.

Estimating the coefficients. An AR model is a regression on lags, so least squares works, as the PACF-by-hand cell above showed. statsmodels uses maximum likelihood, which gives nearly the same answer. The estimated coefficients say how much of the value $j$ steps ago carries over into today, so AR models are interpretable.

Limitations of AR models

  • They require the series to be stationary.
  • High-order AR models can overfit the training data and forecast badly.
  • They cannot model nonlinear relationships.
  • Natively they ignore exogenous factors, i.e., other time series relevant to the prediction, such as the temperature when predicting electricity load (Chapters 7 and 8). Extensions such as ARX and ARMAX add exogenous variables, and dynamic regression combines the regression of Chapter 8 with an AR or MA error.

9.4.1The sign convention in statsmodels#

ArmaProcess (and arma_generate_sample) describe a process by its lag polynomials. With the backshift operator $B$ defined by $B x_t = x_{t-1}$, the AR($p$) model reads

$$(1 - \phi_1 B - \phi_2 B^2 - \dots - \phi_p B^p)\, x_t = \varepsilon_t ,$$

so the ar argument is [1, -phi_1, -phi_2, ..., -phi_p]. The leading 1 belongs to the zero lag and is always there, and the AR signs are flipped. Chapter 4 used exactly this when it wrote ArmaProcess(ar=np.r_[1, -phi], ...). Two things do not flip: the MA polynomial we meet in Chapter 10, and the coefficients that ARIMA reports after fitting, which carry the signs of the model equation.

9.4.2AR model identification#

How do we choose the order $p$? From the PACF. In an AR($p$), $x_t$ has a direct link to exactly $p$ past values, so

  • $\phi_{kk} \ne 0$ for $k \le p$, and in particular $\phi_{pp} = \phi_p$;
  • $\phi_{kk} = 0$ for every $k > p$.

The PACF cuts off after lag $p$. To see this, we generate 200 points of the AR(2) process with $\phi_1 = 0.5$ and $\phi_2 = -0.7$,

$$x_t = 0.5\,x_{t-1} - 0.7\,x_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t ,$$

which in the lag-polynomial convention is ar = [1.0, -0.5, 0.7]. The burnin argument discards the first 1000 simulated values, so the sample does not depend on the zero starting values.

In [10]
ar_process = ArmaProcess(ar=np.array([1.0, -0.5, 0.7]), ma=np.array([1.0]))
print(f"stationary: {ar_process.isstationary}")

ar_data = ar_process.generate_sample(nsample=200, scale=1, burnin=1000, distrvs=rng.standard_normal)

run_sequence_plot(ar_data, title=r"AR(2) process: $x_t = 0.5\,x_{t-1} - 0.7\,x_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t$", figsize=(12, 3))
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

stationary: True

Here $\phi_1^2 + 4\phi_2 = 0.25 - 2.8 < 0$, so the roots are complex and the series oscillates irregularly around zero. The pseudo-period is $2\pi / \arccos\!\big(\phi_1 / 2\sqrt{-\phi_2}\big) \approx 5$ steps.

Now the two correlograms, with the theoretical values of this AR(2) marked in red:

In [11]
correlograms(ar_data, lags=20, process=ar_process, title="of the AR(2) process")
plt.show()
  • The ACF is a damped oscillation that stays significant for several lags. The coefficients are not visible in it: theory gives $\rho(1) = \phi_1/(1-\phi_2) \approx 0.29$ (not $0.5$) and $\rho(2) = \phi_1\rho(1) + \phi_2 \approx -0.55$ (not $-0.7$).
  • The PACF has, besides the spike at lag 0 that is always there, two significant lags and then nothing:
    • a positive spike at lag 1, equal to $\phi_{11} = \rho(1) \approx 0.29$;
    • a negative spike at lag 2, equal to the coefficient itself: $\phi_{22} = \phi_2 = -0.7$.
  • So the process has a memory of length 2: once the last two values are known, older values add nothing. Most of the correlation in the data is explained by the previous two time steps. To fit an AR model to these data we would choose $p = 2$.

The pair of patterns is the AR fingerprint: the ACF tails off gradually (geometrically, or as a damped oscillation like here) while the PACF cuts off after lag $p$. The sample values scatter around the red theoretical marks; that scatter is what the confidence band is there to absorb.

Try it yourself

  • Change the ar coefficients of ArmaProcess above: modify them, add a third lag, or remove the second. Check isstationary first; a nonstationary choice will explode.
  • Then see how the PACF changes. Does the cut-off always land on the order you chose? How about $\phi_2 = -0.1$?

9.5Summary#

  1. The ACF measures direct plus indirect correlation with past values; the CCF does the same between two series and finds lead-lag delays.
  2. The PACF keeps only the direct part: $\phi_{kk}$ is the last coefficient of a regression on $k$ lags.
  3. Correlation functions see only linear relationships, and they are meaningful only for stationary series.
  4. Autoregressive models regress the series on its own past. An AR($p$) is stationary when all roots of $1 - \phi_1 z - \dots - \phi_p z^p$ lie outside the unit circle ($|\phi_1| < 1$ for AR(1)); an AR(2) with complex roots produces pseudo-cycles.
  5. Mind the statsmodels convention: ar = [1, -phi_1, ..., -phi_p], i.e., the AR signs are flipped.
  6. The AR fingerprint is an ACF that tails off and a PACF that cuts off. Choose the order $p$ as the last significant lag of the PACF.
